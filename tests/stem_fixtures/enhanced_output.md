# Chapter 3. Relativistic Mechanics
## 3.1 Mass-Energy Equivalence
The most famous equation in modern physics defines the equivalence
between mass and energy. The relation was first derived by Einstein
in his 1905 paper and reads as follows:
E^2 = p^2c^2 + (mc^2)^2
(3.1)
where p is the relativistic momentum of the particle, c is the speed
of light in vacuum, and m is the invariant rest mass. This continuity equation must be preserved under any Lorentz transformation.
## 3.2 The Schrödinger Equation
In quantum mechanics, the time-dependent Schrödinger equation is:
iℏ ∂ψ/∂t = Ĥψ
(3.2)
A canonical integral that appears throughout statistical mechanics:
∫ \frac{∞}{0} e^{−x} dx = 1
(3.3)
The exponential function admits the Taylor series expansion:
e^x = ∑\frac{∞}{n=0} x^n/n!
(3.4)
## 3.3 Notation in Chemistry
Subscripts are equally important in chemical formulae. Water is
written as H_2O, sulfuric acid as H_2SO_4, and
Avogadro's number is N_A = 6.022 × 10^{23} mol^{−1}.
## 3.4 Greek Symbols in Physics
Common Greek letters: α (alpha), β (beta), γ (gamma), Δ (Delta),
λ (lambda), μ (mu), π (pi), σ (sigma), φ (phi), ω (omega).
Operators: ∇ (nabla), ∂ (partial), ∫ (integral), √ (radical).
